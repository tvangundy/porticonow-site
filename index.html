A plain case for home data

We are storing our lives in the wrong place.

Most of your personal data is not in your house. It is on computers owned by companies you have a login with. That arrangement made sense when computers were expensive and the internet was small. It does not make sense for what comes next.

What is actually happening

When you add something to a grocery list, upload a photo, or send a message through a popular app, that information is copied to a data center. The company runs the software. The company holds the copy. You get access through an account.

That is not a conspiracy. It is the default design of modern software. Build the app in the cloud. Store the data in the cloud. Charge for the service, sell ads, or both.

For a while, this was convenient. You did not have to maintain a server. Your phone synced automatically. Your family could share a calendar without anyone becoming an IT department.

The convenience was real. So was the trade.

Why the current direction fails

When your data lives on someone else's computers, your access is conditional. They can change the terms. They can raise the price. They can shut the product down. They can lock you out of an account because their automated system flagged something. They can train models on your family's photos because the license you clicked ten years ago allows it.

You do not need to believe anyone is evil for this to be a problem. You only need to notice that your household does not hold a copy of its own history. The photos from your child's first year. The messages with a parent who has passed. The list your partner edited last Tuesday. They exist as entries in someone else's database.

That is a fragile way to live in a digital world. Fragile for families. Fragile for communities. Fragile for anyone who expects their records to outlast a product roadmap.

What we need instead

We need to collect our own data at our own homes.

Not as a political statement. Not as nostalgia for the 1990s. As infrastructure — the same way we expect running water, electricity, and a roof that keeps rain out. Your personal data should have a physical home that you control. A computer in your house, or your office, where the default copy lives.

Sharing should be deliberate. You invite someone. They get access to what you chose. The internet helps with connection. It should not be the vault where your life is kept.

This is not about refusing the cloud entirely. Coordination still matters. Accounts still matter. Discovery still matters. But the center of gravity moves. Your home holds the data. The cloud helps people find each other and connect.

What this is not

It is not paranoia. You can use commercial services and still keep copies at home. The point is where the authoritative copy lives, not whether you ever touch the internet again.

It is not only for engineers. You should not need to read a manual about Kubernetes to keep a family grocery list on hardware you own. The tools have to meet people where they are — start simple, grow when you are ready.

It is not a VPN pitch. A VPN puts your laptop on a network. It does not give your family a shared place for apps and data that stays in your house.

It is not buying a NAS and calling it done. Storage is one piece. You need software that runs at home, sharing that works for ordinary people, and a path that does not punish you for not being a sysadmin.

Why now

AI systems are hungry for data. Platforms are consolidating. Accounts are the gate for more of daily life — health records, school communication, home devices, work documents. The cost of having no local copy goes up every year.

Homelab hobbyists have known this for a long time. They are not wrong. They are early. The rest of us need a version that does not require a weekend project every time something breaks.

The direction of the industry is clear: more centralization, more dependence, more of your life in accounts you do not control. The direction we need is the opposite. Data at home. Sharing by invitation. Software that treats your house as a first-class place to run.

What comes next

A small number of people are building toward this. Portico Works is one of them — software that lets you start in the cloud today and move your data home when you are ready, without throwing away sharing or asking your family to become administrators.

We are not asking you to trust a slogan. We are asking you to look at where your data lives today and decide if that is where you want it in ten years.
